Game Mechanics

Game mechanics are the most important part of any game. They are the rules that define what players can do. Some completely warp how the game is played, while others expand upon that experience.


There are many ways to define/categorize game mechanics. One useful framework, made by game designer Charmie Kim, separates a game's mechanics into four layers:

  • Core mechanic(s) are systems which make up the game's primary gameplay loop.
  • Secondary mechanics aren't essential for the core gameplay loop, but add depth to it.
  • Progression mechanics determine how the player's/game's state changes over time.
  • The narrative gives context and meaning to the player's actions and the game's mechanics within its world.

As an example, here is how I would fit a few mechanics from League of Legends into this framework:

LoL's core mechanics

Moving, attacking, using abilities, and taking/dealing damage are examples of interactions that repeatedly form the game's core combat loop.

Some of LoL's secondary mechanics

Recalling, warding, pinging, and taking objectives support the core gameplay while giving it strategic depth.

LoL's progression mechanics

Items, leveling up, role quests, and map pressure create a layer of progression in a way that's meaningful for the rest of the game.

LoL's narrative layer

League's worldbuilding and characters improve the game feel in different ways.


The core mechanics diagram works great. However, I've recognized that game mechanics are still an inconsistently defined concept; There is no universally accepted framework for categorizing them.

I would personally argue that it'd be more useful to categorize game mechanics based on their specific purpose (systemic, traversing, narrative, reward, feedback, meta (inventory), etc) instead of forcing them into a fixed hierarchy. Despite that, I still support Charmie Kim's diagram as it's currently the most practical design tool for game mechanics I've found.



Core mechanics I've designed & implemented

Moving Elara around with a hairdryer

I designed a game around the theme "Bubbles". The core gameplay loop of my game involved playing around the hairdryer as your only tool to move Elara.


Environmental hazard

For a space invaders type game, I designed various semi-random hazard mechanics such as asteroids, supernovas or solar rays.

I consider this a core mechanic because they directly participate during the entire core gameplay loop while they are just as important to dodge as normal enemies.


VR physiotherapy exercisebar

I designed an exercisebar which could support various types of exercises in VR.



Secondary mechanics I've designed & implemented


VR Physiotherapy realistic bow

I designed a mechanic where your left controller is the bow, and your right controller is your hand grabbing on to a piece of string. The farther you pull back (good posture), the stronger your shot.

Looking back, I should've probably made a feature where you could swap the bow to your other hand.


Hiding eyeballs

Eyeball enemies that hide from the player by using meatshields at reasonable distance while trying their best to respect other eyeball enemies.

If there aren't any meatshields left, they would for some reason gather the courage to rush towards the player. This was the very first game I had ever made; Had I designed this today, I would've made them scatter instead, and then fly away from the player if the player gets too close while respecting the boundaries of the game (not fly offscreen).


Combining items to become a stronger samurai

You could combine items by match-3'ing them to make the item stronger. Upgrading the gloves allowed the player to reach further on the board.



Creating fun mechanics takes a lot of creativity and refinement. It gets very difficult sometimes, but it's a process that I really enjoy.

When designing these game mechanics, I think it's important to design them while considering how they fit within the larger systems of the game: Game systems/Game loops. No mechanic is isolated, they work together.
