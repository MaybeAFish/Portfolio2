Stylization

When we received the game, it was lacking the following:

  • Clarity
  • Visual identity
  • Cohecency

I wanted to challenge myself on these problems by iterating on the HUD, improving the game's visibility and designing a new visual style for the game.


Scene clarity

One of the first things I worked on was making the game scene clearer.

The game was extremely dark, and unfortunately this wasn't helping either the gameplay or the visual presentation. Important objects, enemies and environmental details were all hard to understand. During one of the playtests I held, I even noticed someone trying to shoot a dark statue thinking it was an enemy.

I wanted Doomed to remain dark and gloomy, but the game could achieve that atmosphere in other ways without sacrificing its readability.


First HUD iteration

Afterwards, I started working on the HUD. The original HUD communicated a lot of information, but not all of it was useful to the player.

My design reasoning:

You immediately spawned into the game with every weapon and ability, alongside a mana system, a random score, and a timer. This made the HUD very busy before the player even had a chance to understand the game.

I decided to remove the timer and score because they added information without contributing much to the experience. The score was just visual noise, but I'd argue that the timer in aprticular was worsening the experience substantially more.

The timer made it feel as if the player was supposed to speedrun the game. Boomer shooters aren't a genre where I have much familiarity with, so I went searching online to figure out which feeling and experience our players want to get out of the game. After finding an online thread with the title: "What gives you fun in boomer shooters?", the top comment entailed:

"It gives me fun to explore the levels, after I cleared an area,of enemies, I'm checking out the cool architecture, the decorations, try to interact with the environment, and look for secrets."

I wanted the player to have time to appreciate their kills and explore the environment rather than immediately feeling pressured to finish asap.

I had also replaced the mana system with the already existing ammo and we changed the progression structure so the player would unlock weapons and abilities gradually throughout the game. This meant the HUD no longer needed to communicate every possible ability and weapon from the moment the player spawned.

Second HUD iteration

After working on some combat mechanics, I returned to the HUD and continued iterating on its layout and feedback.

During playtesting, I discovered that players often didn't realise that the melee was available to them.

So, I decided to make the melee ability directly visible in the HUD. I put it on the left side, and moved the dash ability UI with it. I believe that this created a better visual balance, otherwise most information would be concentrated on the right side of the screen.

And, most importantly, I added the keybinds underneath them. People would otherwise have to check random buttons to figure the keybinds out or give up on these abilities.

The original dash icon was also a random placeholder. I had created custom .svg icons for both the dash and the new melee icon.

This was a completely new workflow for me at the time, but it gave the HUD a more consistent visual language.

Here are my first sketches, made in Canva.

I had quickly noticed that line-art didn't really fit in to our hud. Filling those icons made more sense for the already existing fill animation representing your dash charge timer.

And aside from the filling animation, I had created two "chevrons", each indicating a charge to communicate that the player has two dashes available, as well as some feedback when one of those charges is restored.

And finally, I added an ability icon with a pizza timer and a right-click icon to the HUD. This UI will only appear when the currently equipped weapon actually has access to that ability.


Visual identity

I was basically fixing a bunch of tiny visual problems at a time. But once the HUD and readability were in a better place, I started looking at the bigger visual problem.

I spoke with a teacher who has, give or take, a gazillion years of game development experience and has played more boomer shooters than my age.

He gave me some feedback, telling me that Doomed could benefit from having its own art style. This brought me back to the same problem I had identified earlier with visual identity.

I had already been doing shader research, so I started experimenting with ways to give the existing assets a more coherent look without having to replace all of the project's art.

I developed a cel shader that I applied individually to ALL materials. This encouraged assets to follow a consistent combination of albedo, normal, and ORH maps rather than relying on a single post-process effect over the entire scene which wouldn't be performant or look as good. Forcing the assets to only use these 3 maps also created a greater visual identity to the game, as some models that used more than those three texture maps would otherwise have a lot more detail than other assets.

But to be honest, a lot of the models are asset flips, and either didn't have a normal map, didnt have an ORH map, or were relying on other maps. These models would look off next to the rest of the environment. You can sometimes notice this while crossing different sections in the map with assets from different publishers.

My system would work substantially better if it was designed around our own assets, that would use the same sets and style of albedos and normals.

Aside from the cel shaders, I had also developed some outline shaders, together with a boil effect version that is applied on the viewmodel, enemies and interactables. These outlines are used as an overlay shader, and would make models pop instead of blending together while creating unreadable noise.

Despite the inconsistent shader usage between assets from different publishers, I believe these shaders to have made great improvements on the game's visual identity.


Experimentation

The final result didn't come from the first try. I had to experiment quite a bit to find a style that worked for both the game's depressing atmosphere and its gameplay.
